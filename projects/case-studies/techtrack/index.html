01

The problem

TechTrack is the first full case study in EN.705.603 (Creating AI-Enabled Systems), which I am taking at Johns Hopkins in fall 2026 as part of my MS in Artificial Intelligence. The scenario is a fleet of warehouse robots that have to find and label twenty kinds of objects (people, forklifts, pallets, boxes, safety gear, vehicles, signs) in a 30 frames per second camera feed, on CPU only, with a human review loop that turns the model's mistakes into retraining data.

The robot's planner stops or reroutes on every box it receives. A missed person is the most expensive error the system can make, and I set every threshold in the analysis against person recall first.

02

What I built

I built the inference service in Python and OpenCV, then the evaluation and retraining pieces around it.

  • Preprocessing, a YOLOv4-tiny detector, and non-maximum suppression (NMS), all running on CPU
  • IoU matching, per-class precision-recall curves, and 11-point mean average precision (mAP)
  • The YOLO loss, split into its localization, objectness, no-object, and classification terms
  • A hard negative miner that ranks images by that loss
  • Augmentation transforms (blur, brightness, contrast, flips) and the Docker packaging
03

The design analysis

Both candidate models arrived trained, so the analysis tunes the system around them rather than the networks. I ran each model once over the 9,525-image logistics dataset, cached every candidate box above 0.01 objectness, and made each later experiment a re-read of that cache. An evaluation went from two minutes to about two seconds on my laptop.

  1. Model selection

    Model 2 led model 1 on mAP (0.583 against 0.544), was ahead or level on all 20 classes, and kept the lead under every match IoU and score threshold I tried. I set the deployed objectness threshold at 0.25, where person recall stays within 0.02 of its best and the planner gets 3.5 boxes per frame instead of 8.6.

  2. Sampling strategy

    I drew a 5,000-image working sample stratified by the rarest class in each image, using label counts only. It keeps every ladder, gloves, and freight container image. On the ten rarest classes its AP error against the full set is half that of a uniform sample for model 2.

  3. NMS threshold

    A sweep from 0.1 to 0.9 found a flat top between 0.4 and 0.6 that held on the 4,525 images outside the sample and at the score thresholds the service would run, so I kept 0.5 in the middle of the plateau.

  4. Augmentation tests

    Heavy blur cost 0.17 mAP and darkening cost 0.11, while a horizontal flip changed nothing and a vertical flip cost 0.25 (an upside-down aisle is a view the robot never sees, so vertical flips stay out of retraining). Boxes per frame fell 26 to 55 percent under blur and darkness, which makes the box count a cheap online signal that the camera or the lighting has changed.

  5. Hard negative mining weights

    I scored all 5,000 images under seven sets of loss weights and compared the 500 hardest images each one selects, then repeated the ranking with the loss divided by the number of objects in the image.

The worst class is not a threshold problem

Wood pallets have the most labeled boxes in the dataset and one of the lowest APs (about 0.3), and no NMS setting moves them. When I measured the best candidate box for each pallet, only 41 percent had one at the 0.5 match threshold and 22 percent had nothing overlapping at all. That is a labeling and retraining problem, and the design routes it to the human review queue.

The NMS curve is lopsided

Setting the NMS threshold too low cost 0.03 mAP from merged neighbors. Setting it too high cost 0.10 from duplicate boxes on every object. Sparse classes like car and gloves ignore the setting until 0.8, while crowded ones like person and helmet need at least 0.3.

Summed loss mines crowds

Ranked by total loss the hardest 500 images average 20 objects each against 4 for the dataset (mostly pallet stacks), and the loss weights I tried keep 90 to 99 percent of that set. Dividing the loss by the object count selects an almost entirely different set (25 images in common) led by fire and the other low-AP classes. I would make that switch the miner's first configuration option.

05

Limits and notes

The implementation passed all 41 autograder tests, and the design report scored 99 out of 100. The dataset is built from mosaic tiles rather than real warehouse frames, so the crowding and augmentation results describe that construction as much as a live camera. The timing (about 13 ms per frame against a 33 ms budget) is one laptop CPU, not the robot's.

Course repositories stay private under the university's rules, so this page summarizes my results and does not link the code.
